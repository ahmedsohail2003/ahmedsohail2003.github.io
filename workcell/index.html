WorkCell: five ways to teach one simulated robot arm

One simulated arm, one job (pick up a block and put it in a tray), five ways of teaching it, and the one I would choose.

In 60 seconds

I taught one simulated robot arm the same job, pick up a block and put it in a tray, five different ways, and scored each one. Copying 150 recorded demonstrations: 90% success. Fine-tuning a pretrained robot model on 160 demonstrations: 90%. Hand-written geometry with no training at all: 20 of 20, but only for an object whose shape is programmed in. Reinforcement learning with no demonstrations: solved a simpler reach-a-point task after 8,000 practice moves, but only partly solved grasping. Rewriting the geometry's final step in C++ for ROS 2, the software real robots run on: the same results to within 0.001 mm, about three times faster. There is no single winner; the right method depends on whether you know the part's shape, can record demonstrations, or can only describe a score. I also checked the 2023 research paper the imitation model comes from, claim by claim: the claims I could test held up, except that its recommended averaging trick fails when the demonstrations show more than one way of doing the task.

The five approaches, and the conclusion

Which is the best way to teach a low-cost robot arm to pick up a block and put it in a tray? I had a simulated SO-ARM100 (a 3D-printed arm with five joints and a two-finger gripper that costs about $150 in parts, which is why hobbyists and students use it for robot-learning experiments), one job, and no idea which of the standard teaching methods was best. So I tried five of them on the same arm in the same simulated work cell (one robot station on a production line). The two methods that learn from recorded demonstrations, A and B below, got the identical task and the identical test; C, D and E each needed their own setup, and I say where they differ. The simulation ran in MuJoCo, a physics simulator: software that calculates how the arm, the block and the tray move, collide and come to rest, and draws what each camera would see.

In A and B, the model that controls the arm gets two camera images (one camera in front of the table, one on the arm's wrist) plus the arm's six joint readings (the five arm joints and how far the gripper is open), and nothing else; it is never told where the block is. A test attempt puts the block at a new random spot and counts as a success if the block ends up in the tray and the gripper pulls away within 16 seconds. Every ACT model was scored on two such tests, one of 20 attempts and one of 50; SmolVLA on the 20-attempt test only. Below, the 50-attempt figures are the headline numbers, and the 20-attempt figures appear wherever two models are compared on that test.

  1. AImitation learning: copy recorded demonstrations. A demonstration is a recording of the task being done correctly: the two camera images, the six joint readings and the motor command (a set of six target angles, one per joint plus the gripper), 15 times a second. I recorded them with a scripted controller: a program I wrote that reads the block's true position from the simulator, which the model being trained never gets, and moves the gripper to the block along a fixed path. The model, called ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers), is trained to output the same motor commands the recordings contain when it sees the same camera images. I trained it four times, on four sets of demonstrations. Trained on 100 demonstrations recorded with the lighting, colours and physics always the same, it succeeded in 37 of 50 test attempts (74%). Trained on 150 recorded under randomly varied lighting, colours, block weight and friction, it succeeded in 45 of 50 (90%) on the same fixed-condition test. Trained on 160 demonstrations of which 20 (12.5%) deliberately miss the block on the first grab and then re-grab it, it succeeded in 31 of 50 (62%). A fourth set, 210 demonstrations with extra ones in the area where the 150-demonstration model kept failing, fixed the failures there but created new ones elsewhere (44 of 50).

  2. BA pretrained robot "foundation model", fine-tuned. The base model was SmolVLA, a model with 450 million parameters (the adjustable numbers inside a model that training sets; more parameters means a bigger model), published by Hugging Face, the website where machine-learning models and datasets are shared. Hugging Face pretrained it on a large collection of community recordings, mostly from real arms of the same SO-100 family as mine. It takes camera images, the arm's joint readings and a text prompt (here always the sentence "Pick up the red block and place it in the blue tray") and outputs motor commands. Fine-tuning means continuing its training on my own demonstrations; I did that on a free Kaggle GPU for about 8 hours, leaving the parts of the model that read the images and the text unchanged (frozen) and adjusting only the part that outputs motor commands. Before fine-tuning it succeeded in 0 of 20 test attempts: a model trained on recordings of real arms does not work in a simulated cell with cameras in different places until it is fine-tuned. After fine-tuning on the 100 fixed-condition demonstrations, 11 of 20 (55%). After fine-tuning on the 160 demonstrations that include the miss-and-re-grab ones, 18 of 20 (90%). On the same 20-attempt test, those 160 demonstrations had lowered ACT from 13 of 20 (65%) to 10 of 20 (50%). So the same demonstrations that pulled ACT down pushed SmolVLA up.

  3. CHand-written geometry: no learning at all. Instead of the two colour cameras, C uses a depth camera above the table, which records for every pixel both its colour and its distance from the camera, so the program can convert the image into a list of 3D points on the surfaces it sees. The scene has three blocks of the same size and shape, one red and two in other colours. The program finds and removes the table, a flat plane (the algorithm is RANSAC), splits the remaining points into separate objects (DBSCAN), keeps the red one, and fits the known shape of the block, a 24 mm cube, to those points (ICP) to calculate where the block is and how far it is turned. I wrote all of that maths myself in about 1,030 lines of Python using only NumPy, its numerical library, not a ready-made point-cloud library, and I calibrated the camera's position myself, to within 0.32 mm of its true position. Result: 20 of 20 successful picks in C's own test (a new random layout of the three blocks each attempt), with the block located to within about half a millimetre of its true position. The catch: it only works because I typed the block's exact shape and size into the program, and because the simulated depth camera has no noise. A different object means new code, and a real camera would need noise handling I have not written.

  4. DReinforcement learning: the arm learns from a score, with no demonstrations. Here there are no recordings to copy. The arm starts by moving at random in the simulator, and after every move it receives a score, the reward. A learning algorithm then adjusts the arm's controller (a small neural network that turns the arm's joint readings and the block's position into the next motor command) so that moves which earned higher scores become more likely; repeated over hundreds of thousands of moves, the controller improves. For this part I changed the setup: the controller reads the position of the target or block from the simulator instead of seeing camera images, and it gets two simpler tasks, because the question here is how much practice each method needs. Reach: move the gripper tip to within 2 cm of a target point; the reward was minus the distance from the tip to the target (so the score rises as it gets closer), minus a small penalty for large motor commands, plus 1 for arriving. Lift: grasp the block and hold it more than 7 cm above the table. On reach I compared two standard algorithms, PPO and SAC, against a planner I wrote: three small neural networks learn to predict where the arm's joints will be after a given command, from 8,000 random moves, and before each move a search tests 256 candidate sequences of 15 moves against those predictions, executes the first move of the best sequence, and plans again. My planner (one training run) reached the target in 20 of 20 test attempts after those 8,000 moves. PPO reached 20 of 20 on all three of its runs at its 300,000-move budget (its learning curve levels off around 250,000), and SAC reached 19 or 20 of 20 on each of three runs at its 100,000-move budget (levelling off around 50,000). Lift I gave only to SAC. Its reward went through four versions: version one paid a one-time bonus for lifting past 7 cm and ended the attempt there, so the arm learned to hover the block just below 7 cm and keep collecting the per-move points instead; version two paid for every move above 7 cm with nothing to discourage going higher, so the arm yanked the block as high as it could; version three paid too little for the first few centimetres, so the arm often never lifted at all. The final version pays steeply for the first 7 cm of lift, then 5 points for every move the block is held above 7 cm, minus a penalty above 14 cm, plus small amounts for getting the gripper near the block and closing on it. With it, SAC succeeded in 18, 15 and 11 of 20 attempts (90%, 75%, 55%) across three separate training runs.

  5. EC's final step, rewritten in C++ and run on ROS 2. A real robot runs many separate programs at once (one reading the camera, one driving the motors, one planning routes), and ROS 2 is the messaging system those programs use to pass data to each other. It is the industry standard, and ready-made mapping and navigation programs exist for it. I rewrote the final step of C's block-locating maths, the rotation search and the ICP fit, in C++ (Python is convenient; C++ is faster), including my own 120-line KD-tree, the data structure that makes finding the nearest point among thousands fast, rather than pulling in a large point-cloud library. I wrapped it as a ROS 2 service: another program sends it the block's 3D points and gets back the block's position and rotation. On the same 12 saved sets of block points, the C++ version calculated the same block position as the Python version to within 0.001 mm on every one, in 4.33 ms per set instead of 14.0 ms, 3.3 times faster on average. A production line also has to move parts between cells, so I also ran a simulated wheeled robot (a TurtleBot3) under ROS 2's ready-made mapping and navigation programs: it built a map of a room from its spinning laser range-finder while a small exploration program I wrote drove it around, then drove itself to 6 of 6 target points on that map.

The decision rule

No single winner; it depends on what you have. If the objects are a known shape and size that you can type into the program, as the 24 mm block was, use the hand-written geometry (C): it needs no training data, locates the object to half a millimetre, and when it fails you can see why by looking at the 3D points it was given. If the objects vary but you can cheaply record a few hundred demonstrations that all do the task the same way, use imitation learning (A), and record the demonstrations under randomly varied lighting, colours, block weight and friction, which raised ACT's success from 74% to 90%. If your demonstrations mix ways of doing the task (some grab the block first time, some miss and re-grab), or you might later want to give the arm a different job by changing its text prompt, use the fine-tuned foundation model (B): of the two models trained on mixed demonstrations, it was the one whose success rate went up. (Whether it actually responds to a different prompt is something I have not tested.) If nobody can demonstrate the task at all, reinforcement learning (D) is the fallback; I never gave it the full pick-and-place task, and on the two simpler tasks it fully solved reaching a point but managed grasp-and-lift in only 55% to 90% of attempts depending on the run. Whichever approach you choose, it still has to run on ROS 2 (E) to go into a real robot. For a cell handling parts of a known shape, I would choose C, running as a C++ program under ROS 2.

ACT, the imitation model in A, comes from a 2023 robotics paper (Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn). I ran the paper's training method on a much cheaper setup: one arm instead of two, two small cameras instead of four, and a free or laptop GPU. I got 65% to 90% success from 100 to 150 demonstrations, in line with the 86% the paper reports on its own simulated block task. The paper also recommends a technique applied while the trained arm is running, not during training. ACT does not output one motor command at a time: each time it looks at the cameras it outputs a plan of the next 45 commands, and normally it follows the first 15 before looking again and making a new plan. The technique, called temporal ensembling, makes a new plan after every single command and averages the overlapping plans, so each command actually sent is a blend of several recent plans. For the model trained on the fixed-condition demonstrations, this worked as the paper says, raising success from 13 to 15 of 20 (65% to 75%). But for the model trained on the demonstrations that include miss-and-re-grab, a case the paper never tested, the averaging lowered success from 10 to 8 of 20 (50% to 40%), because averaging a plan that says "lift now" with a plan that says "go back and re-grab" produces a movement that does neither. So: switch the averaging off whenever the demonstrations contain more than one way of doing the task, or use a model such as SmolVLA, which coped with them; my explanation, not proven, is that its output layers can hold "lift" and "re-grab" as separate options instead of blending them.

01The problem

The robot is the SO-ARM100 described above. I did not build one. I used the simulator model of it published by DeepMind in MuJoCo Menagerie, their collection of accurate simulator models of real robots, so the joints and their ranges of motion match the physical arm, the motor commands are the same six target angles the physical arm's servos take, and the demonstrations are saved in the same LeRobot format used for recordings from the physical arm. Whether anything trained here would work on the real arm is untested; nothing has run on hardware.

The task: a red block, a 24 mm cube, appears at a random spot on the table, and the arm has to pick it up and drop it in a blue tray. In the main comparison (A and B) the model controlling the arm sees only two camera images (a camera in front of the table and a camera on the arm's wrist, each 224 by 224 pixels) and the arm's six joint readings. It is never given the block's position.

Why simulate: MuJoCo is fast enough to run thousands of attempts on a laptop, which is what made a five-way comparison affordable. Recording 620 demonstrations, running hundreds of test attempts, and letting the reinforcement-learning runs practise for up to 500,000 moves cost nothing but computer time.

Why it matters: a work cell has to be re-taught every time the part, the mounting that holds it, or the lighting changes, and how you teach it decides what you pay. On a real cell, a person driving the arm to record demonstrations costs hours (here a script did it, which is why it cost nothing). A pretrained model costs GPU hours (about 8 on a free GPU here). Hand-written geometry costs an engineer's time (about 1,030 lines of maths here). Reinforcement learning costs simulator time (up to 500,000 practice moves here). And moving any of them onto ROS 2 costs integration time, which turned out to be the largest cost of all.

02The experiment

The five approaches are lettered A to E throughout: A imitation learning, B the fine-tuned foundation model, C hand-written geometry, D reinforcement learning, E the move onto ROS 2.

A and B were tested identically: the same simulated arm, block and tray, the same range of possible block starting positions, and the same test. One test attempt puts the block at a position taken from a fixed list of random positions, the same list for every model, none of which appeared in training, and counts as a success if the block ends up in the tray and the gripper pulls away from it within 16 seconds (240 motor commands at 15 per second). Every ACT model ran a 20-attempt test and a 50-attempt test: the 20-attempt test was the original protocol, and the 50-attempt test was added for more reliable headline numbers. SmolVLA ran the 20-attempt test only. Because ACT gives the same output for the same input, two ACT models tested on the same list can be compared attempt by attempt: I can say which starting positions one model failed on and the other did not, not just how many.

C uses the same arm and block but a depth camera above the table in place of the two colour cameras, and a different scene: three blocks of the same size and shape, one red (the target) and two in other colours, with its own 20-attempt test. D changes the question to how much practice a method needs when there is nothing to copy, so it reads the simulator's exact numbers (joint readings and the position of the target or block) instead of camera images and uses two simpler tasks (reach a target point; grasp the block and hold it up), scored on 20 fixed test attempts. E trains nothing: it compares the final step of C's Python code with my C++ rewrite on 12 saved sets of block points, and separately runs a wheeled robot in Gazebo, a second simulator that connects to ROS 2, because a production line also has to move parts between cells.

Hardware: most of the training ran on a free GPU from Kaggle, Google's data-science website, which gives about 30 free GPU hours a week. One ACT model was trained on my laptop's 6 GB GPU, and the SmolVLA tests ran there too. The reinforcement-learning runs used a local GPU for SAC and my planner and a CPU for PPO. ROS 2 ran under WSL2, the Linux virtual machine built into Windows, on the same laptop. Total cloud spend: zero.

Statistics: each ACT and SmolVLA model was trained once; a second training run with a different random seed (the setting that makes a run repeatable) would be an independent check, and I did not run one. With 20 attempts one success is worth 5 percentage points, so 13 of 20 (65%) could plausibly be anywhere from 43% to 82% with more attempts. The claim-by-claim comparison with the ACT paper (linked in section 05) gives that range, a Wilson 95% confidence interval, for every headline number. I treat a difference of one or two attempts as noise, and draw conclusions only from large differences, from results that reverse direction (a gain of 10 points becoming a loss of 10), or from attempt-by-attempt comparisons.

03Five approaches, one cell

Each part answers one question, then says what I tried, what happened, and what went wrong. Technical terms are explained as they appear.

A
PART A · IMITATION LEARNING

Copy 100 to 210 recorded demonstrations (ACT, Action Chunking with Transformers)

Can the arm learn the task by copying recordings of it being done, and what kind of recordings make that work or fail?

What I tried

I built the simulated cell first: the MuJoCo scene with the arm, block, tray and two cameras, and an inverse-kinematics solver, the maths that converts "put the gripper at this point, pointing down" into the five arm joint angles that achieve it. Then I wrote the scripted controller that produces the demonstrations. It reads the block's true position from the simulator and moves the gripper through a fixed sequence of positions (above the block, down onto it, close the gripper, lift, over the tray, open, pull away), completing the task in 99 to 100% of recording attempts; failed recordings were discarded. It is the teacher: the model being trained never sees the block's position, only the two camera images and its own six joint readings. Each demonstration (the two camera images, the six joint readings and the motor command, 15 times a second) was saved in the dataset format of LeRobot, Hugging Face's open-source robot-learning library, so that LeRobot's training code could read it and so that it could be published.

Then I trained ACT, the imitation model introduced in the 2023 paper I compare against in section 05. It takes the two camera images and the six joint readings and outputs the next 45 motor commands as one plan (3 seconds of motion), of which the first 15 are executed before it looks again. I trained it four times, changing only the demonstrations: 100 recorded with the lighting, colours and physics always the same (fixed conditions); 160 recorded the same way except that 20 of them (12.5%) deliberately miss the block by about a centimetre on the first grab and then re-grab it; 150 recorded under randomly varied lighting, scene colours, block weight and friction (this is called domain randomization); and 210 randomized ones, about 30% of them with the block placed in the band of the table nearest the arm, where the 150-demonstration model kept failing. Every model was tested on the same list of block positions, on both the 20-attempt and the 50-attempt test.

What happened

The demonstrations, not the model, decided the outcome. The model trained on the 100 fixed-condition demonstrations succeeded in 37 of 50 test attempts (74%). The model trained on the 150 randomized demonstrations succeeded in 45 of 50 (90%) on the same fixed-condition test, so training under randomized conditions cost nothing on the unrandomized test; it added 16 percentage points. When I randomized the test conditions too (15 attempts per condition, so those figures are rough), that model scored 13 of 15 with nothing changed, 13 of 15 with the lighting and colours changed, 13 of 15 with the physics changed, and 15 of 15 with both changed. The paper's plan-averaging technique (temporal ensembling: make a new 45-command plan after every single command and average the overlapping plans; section 05) raised the fixed-condition model from 13 to 15 of 20 on the 20-attempt test. In short: one model design, trained four ways, scored anywhere from 62% to 90% depending only on what it was shown.

What went wrong

Training on the 160 demonstrations that include miss-and-re-grab, which I expected to teach the model to recover from a bad grab, gave 31 of 50 (62%) against the 100-demonstration model's 37 of 50 (74%); on the 20-attempt test the two scored 10 and 13, and switching plan averaging on took the re-grab model from 10 to 8. The reason: at the moment of a grab, the recordings now contain two different correct next moves, lift or go back and retry, and a model that averages what it has seen produces a movement that is neither. The 210-demonstration model fixed the failures near the arm: on a separate probe of 12 grid positions there it succeeded on 12 of 12 where the 150-demonstration model had managed 5 of 12. But on a second 12-position probe at the far side of the table it succeeded on only 4 of 12, failing all 8 positions furthest from the arm, where the 150-demonstration model had managed 12 of 12; and on the 50-attempt test it scored 44 of 50 (88%) against the earlier 45 of 50. The retraining moved the failures instead of removing them. Both of those models (the re-grab one and the 210 one) are published on Hugging Face, labelled as negative results, with a description page saying what went wrong.

B
PART B · FINE-TUNED FOUNDATION MODEL

Fine-tune a pretrained 450-million-parameter model (SmolVLA)

Does a foundation model for robots, pretrained on other recordings, do the same task after fine-tuning on the same demonstrations, and do the re-grab demonstrations that lowered ACT from 74% to 62% help it?

What I tried

Instead of training a model from scratch, I started from SmolVLA, a freely downloadable model with 450 million parameters (the adjustable numbers inside a model that training sets; more parameters means a bigger model), published by Hugging Face. Hugging Face pretrained it on a large collection of community recordings, mostly from real SO-100-family arms, the same family as mine. It takes camera images, joint readings and a text prompt (here always "Pick up the red block and place it in the blue tray") and outputs motor commands. A model this size just fits on Kaggle's free 16 GB GPU for fine-tuning. Fine-tuning here meant continuing its training on my demonstrations for 12,000 training steps (each step shows the model one batch of recorded moments and adjusts it slightly), about 8 hours on a free Kaggle GPU (the 20,000 I planned would have exceeded Kaggle's 12-hour session limit), while leaving the parts of the model that read the images and the text unchanged (frozen) and adjusting only the part that turns them into motor commands. I tested it exactly the way I tested ACT: the same 20 block positions, the same 16-second limit, the same definition of success.

What happened

The unmodified pretrained model succeeded in 0 of 20 attempts: a model trained on real-robot recordings does not work in a simulated cell with cameras in different places until it is fine-tuned. After fine-tuning on the 100 fixed-condition demonstrations it succeeded in 11 of 20 (55%). After fine-tuning, with identical settings, on the 160 demonstrations that include the miss-and-re-grab ones, it succeeded in 18 of 20 (90%), matching the best ACT model. Those same 160 demonstrations had lowered ACT from 13 to 10 of 20 on this 20-attempt test (37 to 31 on the 50-attempt one), so identical data moved the two models in opposite directions.

What went wrong

My explanation is that the part of SmolVLA that generates the motor commands is trained with a method called flow matching, which produces one movement drawn from a range of possibilities, whereas ACT is trained to minimise its average error against the recordings, which pulls it toward the midpoint when two recordings disagree; so SmolVLA can hold "lift" and "re-grab" as two separate options where ACT blends them. That fits the result but is not proven: SmolVLA has 450 million pretrained parameters and ACT has 52 million trained from scratch, so model size and pretraining are alternative explanations I did not rule out. The model accepts a text prompt, but every demonstration used the same sentence, so I have not tested whether it responds to the words at all. Each result is one training run and 20 test attempts, copied by hand from the program's on-screen output rather than from a saved log file. The code in this repository is the fine-tuning notebook and the test script; the simulator, the scripted controller, the demonstrations and the test are reused from Part A.

PART C · HAND-WRITTEN GEOMETRY

No learning at all: locate the block with maths (depth camera and hand-written algorithms)

If I already know the block's exact shape, can maths alone beat the trained models, with no training data?

What I tried

A depth camera above the table records, for every pixel, both its colour and its distance from the camera. Knowing the distance to each pixel lets the program convert the image into a point cloud: a list of 3D points on every surface the camera can see. The scene has three blocks of the same size and shape (24 mm cubes): a red target and green and yellow look-alikes. The program works through four steps, each an algorithm I implemented myself. The conversion from depth image to point cloud is the first. RANSAC then finds the table: it fits a flat plane to small random samples of points, keeps the plane that the most points agree with, and removes those points. DBSCAN splits the remaining points into separate objects: points packed closely together count as one object and a gap starts a new one, so it never needs to be told how many objects there are; the object whose points are red is the target. ICP then fits an ideal 24 mm cube (a set of points on the surface of a perfect cube, not a trained model) to those points: it repeatedly pairs each measured point with the nearest point on the ideal cube and shifts and rotates the cube to fit better, until it stops improving. The result is the block's position and its rotation about the vertical axis (its yaw), which is all the gripper needs, because a cube resting flat on the table can only differ in how far it is turned. The program is also not told where the camera is relative to the arm. I measured that (a camera calibration) by placing a small sphere at 11 known positions in the simulator and solving for the camera position and orientation that best explains where the sphere appeared in each image. All of it is about 1,030 lines of Python using NumPy, the standard array-maths library; the only ready-made routine is SciPy's nearest-neighbour lookup (finding the closest of thousands of points quickly).

What happened

In the full test (depth image in, block in tray out) the arm picked the red block into the tray in 20 of 20 layouts in which all three blocks were placed at random. The block's position was located to within about half a millimetre: 0.58 mm on average over 15 layouts in Part C's own test, and 0.45 mm on average (worst case 0.77 mm) over the 12 saved sets of block points in the benchmark file in the Part E repository, which runs this exact code. For comparison, simply taking the centre of the block's visible points is off by about 8 mm in my test, because the camera only sees the top and the front faces. The rotation was found to within about 6 degrees on average, and the gripper still closes on the block correctly even when the rotation estimate is up to 20 degrees wrong. The camera calibration recovered the camera's true position to within 0.32 mm and its orientation to within 0.011 degrees; the calibration file that shows this is in the repository. Nothing is trained and no GPU is used. The final step, the rotation search and ICP fit, takes about 14 ms per block on a laptop CPU; the table-finding and clustering steps before it are not timed.

What went wrong

ICP needs a starting guess for the rotation. My first way of choosing it used PCA, a standard method for finding an object's longest axis. A cube seen from above has no longest axis (a square is equally wide in both directions), so the "axis" it found was random noise and ICP kept locking onto a fit 45 degrees off. I replaced it with a brute-force check: try 18 candidate rotations, every 5 degrees across the 90 degrees a cube can differ by, keep the one that fits best, and start ICP from there. Two formulas I derived by hand for how rotating the wrist turns the gripper's jaws each had the sign wrong, so the program now measures which way the jaws point before every grasp, by setting the wrist angle in the arm's model and reading where the jaws end up, instead of trusting a formula. And lowering the gripper straight down onto the block sometimes landed the gripper's fixed jaw (one jaw moves, the other does not) on top of it, because there is only a couple of millimetres of clearance between that jaw and the block, so the gripper now comes in from the side. The limitation of the whole approach: it only works because the block's shape and size are known and the simulated depth camera has no noise. A new object means new code, and a real camera would need noise handling I have not written.

PART D · REINFORCEMENT LEARNING

No demonstrations: the arm learns from a score (reinforcement learning)

With no recordings to copy, how much practice does each learning method need, and can trial and error solve a task that involves grasping?

What I tried

Reinforcement learning works like this: the arm starts by moving at random in the simulator, after every move it receives a score (the reward), and a learning algorithm adjusts the arm's controller (for PPO and SAC, a neural network that picks the next motor command from the joint readings and the position of the target or block) so that the kinds of moves that earned higher scores become more likely. Repeated over many thousands of moves, the controller improves. I set two tasks, both using the simulator's exact numbers (joint readings and block position) as the input rather than camera images. Reach: move the gripper tip to within 2 cm of a target point that changes every attempt (each target is first checked to be reachable). The reward for reach was minus the distance in metres from the gripper tip to the target, so the score rises as the tip gets closer, minus a small penalty for large motor commands, plus 1 for arriving within the 2 cm. Lift: grasp the block and hold it more than 7 cm above the table for the rest of the attempt; its final reward, after four versions (below), pays steeply for the first 7 cm of lift, then 5 points for every move the block is held above 7 cm, minus a penalty above 14 cm, plus small amounts for getting the gripper near the block and closing on it.

On reach I compared three methods. Two are the standard reinforcement-learning algorithms PPO and SAC, taken from Stable-Baselines3, the well-tested library that implements them (PPO learns only from its most recent moves; SAC stores every move it has ever made and keeps learning from all of them, which usually means it needs fewer moves to learn). The third I wrote myself in about 150 lines of NumPy and PyTorch (the standard neural-network library). First, three small neural networks (their predictions averaged) learn to predict where the arm's joints will be after any given command, from 8,000 random moves. Then, before each real move, a planner generates 256 candidate sequences of 15 moves, scores each sequence with the same reward, using the networks' predictions of where the tip will end up instead of running the simulator, keeps the best 32, generates new candidates around those, repeats four times (this search is the cross-entropy method), and executes only the first move of the winning sequence before planning again. A command simply nudges each servo's target position by a known amount, so I compute that bookkeeping exactly and let the networks learn only how the arm actually moves in response. On lift I used SAC alone.

What happened

My planner (one training run, against three each for SAC and PPO) reached the target in 20 of 20 test attempts after learning from just those 8,000 random moves, and stayed at 20 of 20 after three further rounds, each round being a few thousand more moves made by the planner itself, added to the training data, with the networks retrained (the plot of success per round is in the repository; the raw evaluation files are not). SAC reached 19 or 20 of 20 on each of three training runs at its 100,000-move budget, and PPO reached 20 of 20 on all three runs at its 300,000-move budget; the reward-over-time curves in the repository show SAC levelling off around 50,000 moves and PPO around 250,000, with PPO still well below its final level at 100,000. On lift, the final reward got SAC to 18, 15 and 11 successes out of 20 (90%, 75%, 55%) across three separate training runs, holding the block around 16 cm up rather than as high as the arm can reach.

What went wrong

My first lift results were invalid because of a bug in the code that resets the simulator between attempts: every attempt after the first ended after one move. I caught it because the average attempt length came out at exactly 8.45 moves; attempts are capped at 150 moves, so (150 + 19) / 20 is one full 150-move attempt and nineteen one-move attempts. Then the reward had to be rewritten three times. Version one gave points every move for keeping the gripper close to the block and holding it, plus a one-time bonus for lifting the block past 7 cm, after which the attempt ended; the arm learned to hover the block just below 7 cm for the whole attempt, because collecting the per-move points for 150 moves was worth more than the bonus. Version two gave points for every move with the block above 7 cm, and nothing discouraged going higher, so the arm yanked the block as high as it could reach. Version three made the points rise gradually from table level up to 14 cm, which made the reward for the first few centimetres too small to be worth grasping at all, so the arm often never lifted the block. The final version is the one described above. (The three earlier versions are described from my development notes; their code has been replaced.) Each SAC lift run also spent a long stretch grasping the block without lifting it before suddenly improving, at a different point in each run, so one of the three lift runs needed 500,000 moves to get there where the other two needed 300,000. And the prediction networks in my planner cannot tell when one of their predictions is unreliable, and were used only for reach, never for lift.

PART E · ROS 2

Run the block-locating code on ROS 2 (a C++ rewrite, mapping and navigation)

Can the block-locating code move out of a Python script and run on the software real robots use, and does a C++ rewrite give exactly the same results?

What I tried

Parts A to D all ran inside one physics simulator. Real robots run on ROS 2: each part of the robot's software (the camera reader, the motor driver, the route planner, and so on) is a separate program, and ROS 2 is the messaging system they use to send data to each other. It is the industry standard, and ready-made mapping and navigation programs exist for it. I set it up under WSL2 on my laptop. I rewrote the final step of Part C's block-locating code, the rotation search and the ICP fit, in C++ using Eigen, the standard C++ matrix library; the table-finding and clustering steps stayed in Python. I also wrote my own KD-tree (the data structure that makes finding the nearest point among thousands fast) in 120 lines, rather than pulling in a large point-cloud library. The rewrite runs as a ROS 2 service: another program sends it the block's 3D points and gets back the block's position and rotation. To check it, I saved 12 sets of block points produced by Part C's depth camera and clustering steps, along with the true block positions from the simulator, ran both the Python original and the C++ rewrite on all 12, and compared the outputs to the last decimal place.

A production line also has to move parts between cells, so for that I used a simulated TurtleBot3, a small wheeled robot that ships with an official simulation model. A small exploration program I wrote drove it around a simulated room it had no map of for 240 seconds (keep going while the way ahead is clear, turn toward the more open side, with an occasional random turn), while slam_toolbox, a ROS 2 mapping program, built a map from the robot's lidar (a spinning laser that measures the distance to the walls around it). Then Nav2, the ROS 2 navigation program, using AMCL (a program that matches each new lidar scan against the map to work out where the robot is), drove it to six target points: five picked by a script I wrote from the open areas of the map, plus a return to the start.

What happened

For all 12 sets of block points the C++ rewrite produced the same block position as the Python original to within 0.001 mm (the smallest difference the benchmark records: 10 of the 12 were identical to that digit and 2 differed by 0.001 mm), the same rotation, and the same number of ICP iterations. Both versions put the block's centre within 0.45 mm of its true position on average. The C++ version took 4.33 ms per set against Python's 14.0 ms, 3.3 times faster on average (the average of the per-set speed-ups, which ranged from 2.4 to 3.9), on a single CPU core. The navigation run reached all six target points, taking 13 to 49 seconds per point. The benchmark results, the timings of my first slower C++ attempt, and the navigation log are all files in the repository. The demo animations were drawn afterwards from the robot's logged positions and the map, rather than screen-captured, because no screen recorder works with WSL2's graphics.

Under WSL2, most failures are silent: nothing crashes, no error appears, the robot simply does not move. I wrote up nine of them with dates, each with the symptom, the cause and the fix: for example, a speed command sent in an older message format that the robot's simulation bridge silently ignores, a sender and receiver whose delivery settings did not match so no data ever arrived, and a simulator that froze silently while the mapping program kept re-sending its last map, so every process looked alive. The answer

There is no single best way to teach the robot; there is a rule, and each branch of it comes from a number above. Known part shape (here a 24 mm cube): C. No training data, 0.45 to 0.58 mm accuracy, 14 ms per block for the fitting step on a CPU, and when it gets the block wrong you can see which step went wrong (finding the table, separating the objects, or fitting the cube), because every step is ordinary code rather than a trained model. Parts that vary, but a few hundred consistent demonstrations are cheap: A, with the demonstrations recorded under randomly varied lighting, colours, block weight and friction (74% to 90%), and kept to one way of doing the task (adding re-grabs: 74% to 62%). Demonstrations that mix ways of doing the task, or a need to give the cell new jobs later by changing the prompt (whether it actually follows a new prompt is untested here): B, the one of the two demonstration-trained models whose success rate rose on mixed demonstrations (55% to 90%). No demonstrations at all: D, which in my hands solved reaching a point completely and grasp-and-lift only partly (90%, 75% and 55% over three training runs), after four versions of the reward.

Whichever approach wins, it still has to run on ROS 2 (E) next to the robot's other programs, and setting that up cost more time than any algorithm: nine separate silent failures, each written up in the build log. For a factory cell handling parts of a known shape I would choose C, running as the C++ ROS 2 service: it is the cheapest to run (a CPU, 4.33 ms per block for the fitting step), the easiest to check (its answer can be compared with the block's true position, 0.45 mm on average, and the C++ version with the original Python version, to 0.001 mm), and when it fails you can see why by looking at the 3D points it was given, rather than trying to work out what a trained model learned. I would use imitation learning only for parts that cannot be programmed in, and I would record its demonstrations under randomized conditions from day one. The caveat from the five approaches applies to all of it: one task, one arm, simulation only, models trained once.

05Against the paper

ACT was introduced in a 2023 paper by Zhao, Kumar, Levine and Finn (published at the Robotics: Science and Systems conference; arXiv 2304.13705), where it controlled two real arms doing precise, small-scale tasks such as sliding open a ziplock bag and putting a battery into a slot. I picked eight claims from the paper; the table below scores each one. Five of them I could test at least in part, and three I could not. The first is simply whether the published training method works when rebuilt from the paper; it does, and the next paragraph lists what I changed. The other four: that outputting a plan of many future commands at once, rather than one command at a time, is the key idea (the paper's own experiment goes from 1% success with one command at a time to 44% with 100-command plans); that averaging overlapping plans while the arm runs (temporal ensembling) adds a small gain of 3.3 points; that about 50 demonstrations and five hours of training on an ordinary GPU are enough; and that demonstrations recorded by humans are harder to learn from than scripted ones (86% with scripted demonstrations against 50% with human ones, on the paper's simulated block task) because humans are inconsistent and do things more than one way, which the paper gave as the reason but never tested by itself.

What I changed: one arm instead of two, two 224-pixel cameras instead of four higher-resolution ones, 15 motor commands per second instead of 50, scripted demonstrations instead of human ones, a smaller model (52 million parameters instead of the paper's 80 million, with 1 layer instead of 7 in the decoder, the part of the model that turns the processed camera images and joint readings into the plan of 45 commands; 1 layer is the default in LeRobot's version of ACT), and a free cloud GPU or a laptop GPU instead of the paper's 11 GB desktop GPU. Even so, the model trained on 100 demonstrations scored 13 of 20 (65%; the true rate could be 43% to 82%) on the 20-attempt test and 37 of 50 (74%; 60% to 84%) on the 50-attempt test, and the model trained on 150 randomized demonstrations scored 45 of 50 (90%; 79% to 96%), in line with the 86% the paper reports on its own simulated block task. So most of those changes did not matter to the method. The averaging technique also behaved as the paper says on the fixed-condition model: 13 to 15 successes out of 20. I could not afford to retrain the model for each plan length (each is a 60,000-step training run of two to four hours), so I changed only how much of each 45-command plan is executed before re-planning: re-planning after every single command, without averaging, collapsed success to 1 of 20 (in all 19 failures the arm stalled in place until the 16-second limit), while executing all 45 commands without looking again gave 13 of 20, the same as the normal setting. That fits the paper's view that plans help by committing the arm to a course of action, not by reacting faster.

Then I tested the one claim the paper stated but did not isolate. Human demonstrations differ from scripted ones in many ways at once; I changed exactly one thing: 12.5% of the demonstrations deliberately miss the block on the first grab and then re-grab it, so from that moment the recordings contain two correct next moves. ACT's success fell from 13 to 10 out of 20, and the averaging technique, which had helped, now hurt (10 to 8 out of 20): averaging a "lift now" plan with a "go back and re-grab" plan gives a movement that does neither. Trained on the identical demonstrations, SmolVLA rose from 11 to 18 out of 20. So the penalty is not a property of the demonstrations alone; it depends on which model is learning from them. Therefore: the paper's advice to average plans is safe only when the demonstrations contain one way of doing the task; switch it off when they contain retries or alternatives, and prefer a model, like SmolVLA, whose output layers keep alternative plans separate. Three of the paper's claims I could not test without human demonstrations or more GPU time: that the CVAE (an extra part of the model used only during training, which summarises each demonstration's particular way of doing the task so the model can learn from inconsistent human recordings) is essential; that ACT beats four older methods; and that human demonstrations must be recorded at 50 commands per second.

07Open-source fixes, datasets and models

While building Part A on Windows I hit two bugs in LeRobot itself and sent fixes to Hugging Face as pull requests (proposed changes submitted for review). A LeRobot maintainer reviewed one, re-submitted it as their own pull request (#4307, replacing my #4177) with me credited as co-author, and merged it into the library. The bug: LeRobot stores each demonstration's camera images as video and assumed one video-reading library (torchcodec) could be used whenever it was installed; on Windows it usually cannot load, so every attempt to open a dataset crashed. The fix checks whether it loads and otherwise falls back to another library (pyav). Another LeRobot user, not a maintainer, reproduced the same crash on Linux. The second fix (#4174) makes downloading only part of a dataset (selected episodes) work on Windows; it has not been merged yet and is awaiting a maintainer's review. I also tested another contributor's Windows fix (#4121) on a second Windows machine, posted the results and a suggestion as a review comment, and closed my own duplicate fix in favour of theirs.

On Hugging Face I published 4 datasets in LeRobot's format (620 demonstrations, 70,143 camera frames at 15 per second, about 78 minutes of arm motion) and 5 trained models (3 ACT, 2 SmolVLA). Two of the ACT models are the ones that did worse (the re-grab model and the 210-demonstration model), and their description pages say why. The one model not published is the ACT model trained on the 100 fixed-condition demonstrations (the 74% one); it has not been uploaded. The full claim-by-claim comparison with the ACT paper is in the Part A repository, and there is an open request for review of the whole series on GitHub Discussions; as of September 2026 it has no replies, so nothing on this page has been checked by anyone but me. Blunt criticism is welcome there, as a GitHub issue, or by email.

How this was built

The WorkCell series was built in the summer of 2026 (the commit history runs July to August), largely in long sessions with AI coding assistants (Claude and Codex) writing and editing code and text with me. Every experiment, number and mistake on this page was run and checked by me, and I can explain and modify every algorithm in these five repositories; every failure is written up (the "What went wrong" passages above, section 06, and the nine dated failures in Part E's build log). Everything runs in simulation (MuJoCo for Parts A to D and for the block points in Part E's benchmark; Gazebo under ROS 2 on WSL2 for Part E's navigation); nothing has run on a physical robot.
